01 / The Declaration

We Believe in Calixas.

Calixas is not a passing curiosity to be mentioned once and forgotten. It is a vision to return to, examine, argue for, improve, and carry forward. California and Texas are already consequential. The question is whether their shared future can become consequential on an entirely different scale.

The first principle

California and Texas are not identical, and Calixas does not require them to be. The case for union begins precisely because each state has developed different industries, institutions, communities, and traditions. Their differences are not proof that cooperation is impossible.

When someone asks why California and Texas should be considered together, return to the question that defines this movement: why should two states with so much to contribute to a shared future assume that permanent separation is the only arrangement worth considering?

The second principle

Calixas must be more than a name. It must be a proposal that can withstand scrutiny, a vision that can be explained, and a project that respects the people whose lives it would change. Conviction gives the project its identity. Evidence, public consent, and lawful process would determine whether it could become reality.

02 / The Union

What Is Calixas?

Calixas is the proposed unification of California and Texas into a single state, pursued through a lawful process and with the informed consent of the people affected. Its premise is simple: investigate what these states could achieve together that neither could achieve as effectively alone.

I / California

The Pacific Pillar

Technology. Entertainment. Research. Agriculture. International trade. Pacific ports. California contributes a vast collection of industries and institutions to the Calixas vision.

In the Calixas imagination, California is not asked to disappear. It is asked to help build something larger while retaining the regional identity of its communities.

II / Texas

The Lone Star Pillar

Energy. Manufacturing. Agriculture. Logistics. Aerospace. Gulf Coast trade. Texas contributes its own enormous collection of industries and institutions to the same vision.

Texas is not asked to surrender its character. It is invited into a proposal in which its future and California's future are considered part of the same undertaking.

The Calixas proposition: California does not have to become Texas. Texas does not have to become California. A proposed union would need to create a shared political structure capable of respecting both. That is the challenge. That is the work. That is what the name Calixas is meant to evoke.

03 / The Vision

Think Bigger Than the Border.

Calixas begins with a question and demands serious investigation. What happens when we consider California's and Texas's strengths as parts of one potential economic and political system? Every answer must be tested. Every opportunity deserves to be examined.

01 / The Economy

Two Economic Giants. One Conversation.

A combined state would encompass major technology, energy, manufacturing, agriculture, logistics, and entertainment sectors. Calixas calls for serious analysis of whether shared planning and investment could improve productivity and resilience.

The point is not to chant that bigger is always better. The point is to investigate the actual advantages a union might create.

02 / Energy

Build What Neither Can Build Alone.

Texas's energy infrastructure and California's research and technology capabilities could provide opportunities for collaboration in grid modernization, storage, and industrial innovation.

The Calixas question is whether those opportunities could be developed more effectively through a shared framework.

03 / Agriculture

Common Challenges Demand Serious Planning.

Both states face significant agricultural and water-management challenges. Shared research, drought planning, conservation, and infrastructure studies could form part of a Calixas agenda.

A merger would not manufacture water or eliminate drought. It would still have to address geography, environmental limits, and competing demands.

04 / Infrastructure

Connect the Pacific and the Gulf.

California's Pacific connections and Texas's Gulf Coast infrastructure sit within different regional systems. Calixas invites us to investigate coordinated freight, rail, highways, ports, airports, and supply chains.

Two regions. Two coastlines. A shared infrastructure question.

05 / Research

Innovation Without Artificial Boundaries.

Universities, laboratories, businesses, and public institutions could explore partnerships across the two states. Joint programs would help determine which opportunities are real before claims of transformative benefits are made.

06 / Identity

Different Cultures. A Shared Project.

Calixas does not require every Californian to think like a Texan or every Texan to think like a Californian. A credible shared identity would have to make room for disagreement, regional pride, local traditions, and different ways of life.

05 / The Doubts

Conviction Must Face Reality.

Calixas is a proposal for a constitutional transformation, not a magic spell. Believing in the idea means examining the barriers that stand between the idea and reality.

Political differences are real.

California and Texas have different electorates, political cultures, policy priorities, and laws. Those differences would not vanish under a common government. Any credible Calixas framework would need to explain representation, local autonomy, civil liberties, and the handling of political disputes.

The Constitution cannot be wished away.

Combining two existing states would raise substantial constitutional and legal questions. Article IV of the U.S. Constitution contains provisions concerning state consent and congressional approval in relevant state boundary and formation situations. The precise legal process for a California–Texas merger requires qualified constitutional analysis. Calixas cannot simply assume that one vote or one executive order would settle the matter.

Distance is not imaginary.

California and Texas are separated by a vast geographic distance. Government services, representation, transportation, administration, and institutional locations would require careful design. The images may sit next to each other on this page; the states do not sit next to each other on the map.

Economic benefits must be demonstrated.

Combining two economies does not automatically create additional wealth. Calixas would need transparent estimates of transition expenses, tax changes, infrastructure costs, distributional effects, and the impact on households and businesses across both states.

Public consent matters.

The people of California and Texas would have different interests, priorities, and opinions. A proposal affecting their governments must take those views seriously. The project cannot claim a mandate that its citizens have not given it.

Cooperation is the comparison we cannot ignore.

Interstate agreements and joint projects might accomplish some of the goals associated with Calixas without merging the states. A serious investigation must compare these alternatives rather than assume that complete political unification is necessary for every benefit.

The Calixas standard: Repeat the vision. Display image1.jpeg. Display image2.jpeg. Then do the work. A belief that cannot survive honest examination is not a policy proposal. A belief that welcomes scrutiny can become the beginning of one.
